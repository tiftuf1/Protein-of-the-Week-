# What is this protein?
## Green Fluorescent proteins (GFPs) are bioluminescent proteins which when exposed to blue or ultraviolet light, emits a bright green glow.

![Dronpa Rotation](dronpa_rotation.gif)


# Where is it found?
Found by isolating from the jellyfish Aequorea Victoria first discovered in 1960
# What does it do?
The protein is widely used as a biological marker or reporter by being fused into a gene of interest. When the gene of interests expresses this protein, it also produces the GFP, allowing examination under a fluorescence microscope

# Biological Importance of Proper Function
Within the jellyfish, its purpose is still stated with some skepticisms, but is widely believed to acts an energy converter. The jellyfish naturally produces blue bioluminescence, but the GFP absorbs this light, and re-emits it as bright, glowing green light.   
# What happens if it can't do that job in the organism? 
Lacking in such a protein is not lethal, and is only used for visual purposes. 
# Why should scientists or biotechnologists care?
This protein has widespread, important uses in science, with the importance being recognized in 2008 when the Nobel Committee awarded Osamu Shimomura, Marty Chalfie, and Roger Tsien the Chemistry Nobel Prize for this discovery. It is used to label and visualize proteins of interests. 

# Why is the shape this way, whats is it made up of?
There are two alpha helices within the structure, one in the direct middle of a dense beta barrel, while another lays within the bottom of this barrel. The binding sites are found at positions 200, 210, 212
There are no binding pockets in this protein, but instead manufacture their own light-emitting core located inside the beta-barrel by using amino acid residues Gln-Tyr-Gly at positions 62-64 to react with each other and form a chromophore.
# How do scientists exploit this protein?
Scientists exploit this protein by using their on and off switch to track single proteins, completely bypassing traditional diffraction limit of light. Also, by fusing the Dronpa gene to other functional proteins, scientists can easily visually mark and monitor all sorts of behaviors using UV or  cyan light.
