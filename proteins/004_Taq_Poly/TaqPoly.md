# What is this protein?	
This protein is a heat-stable enzyme that builds new DNA strands at high temperatures. It is derived from Thermus aquaticus, a bacteria found in hot springs, surviving temperatures up to 95 C without breaking down. Now in the lab, it is used incredibly often during PCR, medical tests, and cloning. The limitations of using this polymerase instead of others is that it lacks no proofreading.  




# Biological Importance of Proper Function
The normal job of this protein is simply: DNA replication. Now if it cannot do its job, it means absolute death for Thermus aquaticus as it cannot replicate its genetic material and cannot survive in the harsh temperature of the hot spring. 
This specific protein revolutionized modern molecular biology through its widespread use in PCR, acting as the engine, the central ingredient that is used to make billions of copies of a specific DNA sample. One incredibly potent characteristic of this enzyme is the fact that it survives the heating cycle necessary to PCR, allowing scientists to no longer manually add fresh enzymes.

# How do scientists exploit this protein?
Scientists exploit this protein by turning its ability to replicate DNA into a cellular factory to build massive quantities of target DNA. As mentioned before, because the enzyme doesn’t break down during the cycles of PCR, scientists can cycle a single test tube through all these cycles, forcing the protein is rapidly synthesize genes of interests. PCR has three cycles: Denaturation at 95C where the intense heat separated double stranded DNA, annealing at 50-65C where primers bind to specific points of the genes, and extension at 72C where the temperature rises to Taqs optimal working zone, allowing rapid growth of new DNA. Specifically, each cycle doubles the amount of target DNA, and by the end of about 30 cycles, a single piece of DNA has the potential to turn into over a billion copies.
